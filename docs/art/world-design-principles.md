# World and game design principles

How Just Imagine composes worlds and games. These borrow the working habits of theme-park and animation studios: story before scenery, one strong visual magnet, reveals that reward curiosity, and the child as the hero whose choices change what happens. The builder's prompts include `services/builder/src/prompts/world-design.ts`, which is the same rules in prompt form; change both together.

## 1. Story first, scenery second

Every world answers one question before any entity is placed: who is the hero, what do they want, and what is in the way. "A jelly dragon on a cloud" becomes "the dragon wants to reach the rainbow, the gap is too wide, and the child decides how to help". Props exist because the story needs them.

## 2. One magnet, then layers

Give each world a single visual magnet that pulls the eye and the finger: the castle at the end of the street. Place it off-centre on the far third, larger or brighter than everything else. Arrange the rest in three depths, far backdrop, mid play space and near foreground, so the scene reads at a glance and still rewards a second look.

## 3. The child is the hero

The child's choice must visibly change the world within one second: a drop, a bounce, a splash, a door opening. Never build a world where the child only watches. Every world offers at least two meaningful choices with different outcomes, and neither is "wrong"; one is simply more surprising.

## 4. Discovery is staged, not dumped

Hide one surprise that appears only after the first success, the way a ride saves its best scene for the turn. Reveal it with motion and sound, then let the friend explain the science of what just happened in one sentence.

## 5. Something is always alive

At least one ambient motion in every world: a cloud drifting, a fish turning, leaves falling. A still world feels broken to a child. Motion stays gentle and never competes with the magnet.

## 6. Scale tells the story

Make the hero big enough to love and the obstacle big enough to matter. Exaggerate sizes for meaning (a giant friendly snail, a tiny brave ant) and say in the narration when real life is different.

## 7. Characters, not objects

Anything the child touches has a face, a name or a personality where it fits: the pebble is shy, the cup cheers when a ball lands in it. Give the main object a one-line personality the friend can voice.

## 8. Play in three beats

Every game has a setup the child understands in five seconds, a rising middle where difficulty grows in small steps, and a celebration that feels earned. Levels add one new idea each, never two.

## 9. Respect the child's idea

Keep the child's own words in the title and the hero's name. If a wish is impossible in physics, keep it as make-believe and show the real-world version next to it; never replace the child's idea with a safer default without saying so.

## 10. Plus it

Before publishing, add one small extra that makes it delightful: a sparkle trail on the hero, a sound when a star is caught, a cloud that blushes when touched. One, not five.

## 11. Comfort and safety

Cartoon-scary is fine; cruelty, weapons and gore are not. No timers that punish young children; under eight, failure retries instantly with encouragement. Colours stay readable, and nothing flashes more than three times a second.
